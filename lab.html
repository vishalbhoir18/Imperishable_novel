1 · Gravitational lens

Gravity bends light.

Mass curves space, and light follows the curve. Drag the lens across the galaxies behind it and watch them stretch into arcs, split in two and brighten.

Drag the lens

This experiment needs WebGL, which this browser can’t provide. The link below explains it.

What you’re seeing

  • Nothing here is painted on. Every pixel is traced back through the lens with the equation astronomers use for a single compact mass.
  • Galaxies near the lens stretch into arcs around it. Some appear twice: once outside the dashed circle, and again, smaller, inside it.
  • Lensing never creates light. It gathers light from a wider patch of sky, so lensed galaxies look bigger and brighter.

2 · Einstein ring

Line them up, and light makes a ring.

Drag the bright galaxy behind the lens. Off to one side, you see it twice. Bring it into perfect alignment and the two images spread into a ring.

What you’re seeing

  • The dashed circle marks where the bright galaxy really is. What you see is where its bent light seems to come from.
  • The ring’s size is the Einstein radius. It grows with the square root of the lens’s mass. Here the lens is a billion parsecs away and the bright galaxy twice as far.
  • A trillion suns gives an Einstein radius of about two arcseconds: roughly the width of a small coin seen from two kilometres away. Hubble and Webb have photographed many real ones.

3 · Solar gravitational lens

The Sun is a telescope, if you go far enough.

Starlight grazing the Sun bends by less than a thousandth of a degree, so the bent light only meets far beyond the planets. Slide the probe outward to find the focus.

What you’re seeing

  • Distances are squeezed: each step out is far bigger than the last. One AU is about 150 million kilometres.
  • Light passing just over the Sun’s edge meets 547 AU out. Light passing a little farther away bends less and meets farther still, so the focus is a line, not a point. Missions aim for about 650 AU, where the Sun’s glare is easier to block.
  • Engineers have proposed sending a probe there to photograph distant planets. Voyager 1, the farthest probe so far, is about 170 AU away.
  • Switch to the black hole to see why the Eye in Imperishable is so powerful: it bends light far harder, so its focus begins just beyond it.

4 · Light-year

Every light in the sky is a ghost.

Light is the fastest thing there is, and it is still slow on the scale of space. Pick something in the sky to see how old its light is when it reaches you.

What you’re seeing

  • A light-year is the distance light travels in a year: about 9.5 trillion kilometres.
  • The path is squeezed: each tick is ten times farther than the one before.
  • Some stars you see tonight may already be gone. You would not know until their last light arrives.
