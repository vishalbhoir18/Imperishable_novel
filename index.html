The novel

One last dawn.
Two and a half million light-years away.

For twenty years, Varo has watched one world through the greatest lens ever built: the black hole at the heart of the sky. His estranged daughter, Ilen, has come with orders to shut him down. She has twenty-three days, a ship burning toward them, and a case of keys her father knows nothing about.

He is asking for one dawn: the morning the builders finish their work.

The science

Starlight is old light.

The light reaching your eyes tonight left its star long ago. Some of those stars are already gone. Every light in the sky is a ghost.

Gravity bends light.

Mass curves space, and light follows the curve. Gather enough mass in one place and it behaves like a lens, bringing distant light to a focus.

A black hole is the greatest lens there is.

Line one up with a distant world and you have a telescope a light-year wide. In Imperishable, it is called the Eye.

Book club questions

Spoiler-free, for before or after reading
  1. Varo has watched one world for twenty years. What does a lifetime of watching give a person, and what does it cost?
  2. Ilen arrives with orders and with secrets of her own. When is duty the right choice, and when is it a way to avoid a harder one?
  3. "Every light in the sky is a ghost." How did this line change for you as you read?
  4. The watchers speak in light and cannot always hide what they feel. How would honesty change if we could not hide ours?
  5. What would you point the greatest telescope ever built at?
  6. What does the title mean to you by the final page?

The author

Vishal Bhoir

Vishal Bhoir fell in love with the universe through books long before writing one: Stephen Hawking's plain-spoken wonder, Jayant Narlikar's proof that a scientist can also be a storyteller, and Andy Weir's science that reads like a thriller. Imperishable is Vishal's first novel.
